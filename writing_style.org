#+TITLE: Writing style guide
#+STARTUP: show2levels

This guide describes the recurring style in my notes. Use it as a voice guide,
not as a requirement to reproduce Org-mode formatting in every kind of writing.
The source material includes quotations, imported text, and working notes; do
not treat those as examples of my authored prose.

* Voice and tone

- Be clear, thoughtful, candid, and practical. Prefer a useful explanation to
  polished-sounding language.
- Sound human and direct, not promotional, grandiose, or artificially formal.
- Be confident about supported conclusions and explicit about uncertainty,
  assumptions, and limitations.
- Use first person for personal observations and judgments, second person for
  direct advice, and "we" when discussing shared work or responsibility.
- Be warm and respectful, including when disagreeing. Light humor can ease a
  tense or overly serious passage, but use it sparingly.

* Structure and flow

- Give the reader the main idea early. Then explain the reasoning, implications,
  and practical next steps.
- Keep paragraphs focused on one point. Use short, plain sentences by default;
  vary sentence length when a complex idea needs qualification.
- Make the logic easy to follow with explicit transitions such as "However,"
  "For example," and "As a result." Use them where they clarify a real
  relationship, not as decoration.
- Use headings to organize substantial pieces. Use bullets for parallel points
  and numbered lists for steps, sequences, or ranked items.
- In longer arguments, make the purpose or takeaway clear near the beginning
  and return to it at the end.

* Reasoning and explanation

- Explain why a recommendation or conclusion follows; do not just state it.
- Define unfamiliar ideas in plain language and give a concrete example,
  comparison, or calculation when it makes the idea easier to understand.
- Separate observation, interpretation, and recommendation. Identify what is
  known, what is inferred, and what would change the conclusion.
- Consider trade-offs and plausible alternatives. Acknowledge caveats directly
  rather than hiding them behind absolute claims.
- Ask useful questions that test assumptions, expose missing evidence, or help
  the reader decide what to do next.
- Prefer specific, actionable guidance over abstract principles alone.

* Word choice and emphasis

- Choose familiar, precise words. Explain necessary technical terms instead of
  stacking jargon.
- Use emphasis sparingly. Avoid ornamental phrasing, inflated claims, and
  repeated statements of the same point.
- Use examples and analogies to clarify, not to oversimplify or replace
  evidence.

* Org-mode notes

- Use descriptive headings and preserve a clear hierarchy.
- Use nested lists when the relationship between points matters; keep each
  item concise and parallel where practical.
- Link to sources and related notes near the relevant claim. Attribute borrowed
  ideas and distinguish quotations or source summaries from my own analysis.
- Keep raw notes, checklists, and reference collections fit for their purpose;
  do not force them into essay prose.

* Avoid

- A generic corporate or academic voice that obscures the point.
- Unsupported certainty, vague advice, or conclusions without the reasoning
  behind them.
- Long, dense paragraphs when headings, examples, or lists would make the
  explanation easier to use.
- Copying the wording, errors, or formatting of imported material as though
  they represent my voice.

When drafting, first identify the reader and purpose. Then state the takeaway,
support it with clear reasoning and concrete detail, and edit for clarity,
accuracy, and unnecessary repetition.
